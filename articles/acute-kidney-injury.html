Acute Kidney Injury (AKI)

A sudden drop in kidney function that, unlike chronic disease, is often reversible — if it is recognised and treated quickly.

Home/Education/Acute Kidney Injury

Acute kidney injury (AKI) means the kidneys suddenly stop working properly over hours or days. It is different from chronic kidney disease, which develops slowly over years. AKI is common in people who are seriously ill or dehydrated — and with prompt care, the kidneys often recover.

What causes AKI?

Doctors group the causes into three types based on where the problem starts:

Before the kidney

Reduced blood flow — from severe dehydration, blood loss, infection (sepsis), or heart problems.

Within the kidney

Direct damage from certain medicines, toxins, contrast dyes, or inflammation of the kidney tissue.

After the kidney

A blockage to urine flow — such as kidney stones, an enlarged prostate, or a tumour.

Warning signs

  • Passing much less urine than usual
  • Swelling in the legs, ankles or face
  • Tiredness, nausea or confusion
  • Shortness of breath

Common triggers to avoid

Dehydration combined with NSAID painkillers, certain antibiotics, or contrast scans can tip vulnerable kidneys into AKI. If you are unwell and not drinking, ask your doctor whether to pause such medicines temporarily ("sick-day rules").

Treatment and recovery

Treatment focuses on the underlying cause: restoring fluids, treating infection, stopping harmful medicines, or relieving a blockage. In severe cases, temporary dialysis supports the body while the kidneys heal. Many people recover fully, though some are left with a degree of long-term kidney weakness — which is why follow-up testing after AKI is important.

After an episode of AKI

Even after recovery, an episode of AKI raises your future risk of chronic kidney disease. A repeat kidney check a few weeks later helps make sure function has returned to baseline.
